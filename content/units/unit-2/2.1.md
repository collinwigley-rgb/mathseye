# 2.1 — Connect Ratios, Rates, and Unit Rates

Source: Lesson 2-1 student pages (pp. 73–78), 2-1 Additional Practice (pp. 17–18) · Built: 2026-11-03

## In one sentence
Turn any rate into "how much for ONE" (a unit rate), then use it to compare deals and speeds or to scale up to any amount.

## Key concepts and vocabulary
| Term | Plain meaning | Tiny example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ratio | Compares two amounts | 3 boys : 6 girls |
| Rate | A ratio with two *different units* | $81.25 for 5 hours |
| Unit rate | A rate where the second amount is 1 | $16.25 per 1 hour |
| Unit price | A unit rate for cost | $0.21 per card |
| Equivalent ratios | Same relationship, scaled up or down | 3:8 = 6:16 = 9:24 |
| Table of equivalent ratios | Two-row (or two-column) table; do the same × or ÷ to both | see Method 2 |

**Big idea:** more does not mean better. Making 9 of 25 shots is worse than 8 of 20, because 9/25 = 36% and 8/20 = 40%. Compare rates, not totals.

## Problem profile
| Type | Given → asked | Numbers | Format | Share |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Find a unit rate | total for N → amount for 1 | money with cents ($29.45 for 5), large counts (9,780 steps per 5 mi), N from 2 to 25 | fill-in table, typed answer | ~25% |
| B. Compare two or three rates | 2–3 deals or speeds → which is better, and by how much | prices to the cent, packages of 3–16 | word problem + "how much more?" | ~30% |
| C. Scale with a unit rate | rate + new amount → total (or "is it enough?") | days/hours 3–12, multi-step | word problem, often 2 steps | ~20% |
| D. Equivalent-ratio tables | ratio + one new value → missing value | whole-number scale factors (×2, ×3, ×8) | complete the table | ~10% |
| E. Multi-part / assessment | recipes with 2 ingredients across 2 dishes; "select all equivalent rates" | mixed numbers (15½ gal), 2 parts | Part A / Part B, select-all | ~15% |
| F. Critique reasoning | someone's wrong unit price → correct it, name the error | dozens vs. single items | explain | a few |

**Difficulty ladder:** a whole-number unit rate → money with cents → compare 2 → compare 3 → a hidden unit (dozens, months vs. a year, ounces per can × cans per pack) → two steps with "is there enough?"

**Watch for hidden conversions the sheet uses:** dozen = 12, year = 12 months, hour = 60 minutes, and package cost ÷ (cans × ounces per can).

## Methods: ways to attack it

### Method 1: "Get to 1" (divide-to-one)
1. What two things are being compared? Write them as a fraction: **thing I want ÷ "per" thing**.
2. Divide top and bottom by the bottom number. Now the bottom is 1.
3. Need a different amount? Multiply the unit rate by it.
**Memory hook:** "Per means divide. Get to 1, then you're done (or scale up)."
**Check it:** is the unit rate smaller than the total when N > 1? Does the label read naturally ("dollars per hour")?

### Method 2: Ratio table ("do the same to both")
```
Jumps  | 3 | 6  | 9
Meters | 8 | 16 | 24
         ×2   ×3      (same multiplier on top and bottom)
```
1. Put the two quantities in two rows.
2. Ask: "What can I multiply or divide by to reach the number I need?"
3. Do it to both rows. Repeat until you land on the target.
**Best for:** comparing two things at the same total (both animals at 24 meters), and whole-number scale factors.

### Method 3: Compare the same thing (for "which is better")
1. Find each option's unit rate with Method 1.
2. Line them up with the same units.
3. Ask: "Is lower better (price) or higher better (speed, steps, efficiency)?"
4. For "how much more": subtract the unit rates. For "how much will she save": multiply the difference by the total amount.

### Method 4: Bar model (picture it)
Draw one bar for the total, cut it into N equal parts, and ask what one part is worth. Good when the words are confusing.

## Guiding questions (homework help)
1. "What two quantities is this about? Which one is the 'per'?"
2. "What would it be for just ONE of those?"
3. "How do you get from N down to 1?"
4. "Now that you have one, how do you get to the amount they asked about?"
5. "Which is better here, a bigger or a smaller unit rate?"
6. "Read your answer back with units. Does it make sense?"

## Common mistakes
| Mistake | What it looks like | Nudge |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compares totals, not rates | "She made more shots, so she's better" | "Is it fair if they took different numbers of shots?" |
| Divides the wrong way | hours ÷ dollars instead of dollars ÷ hours | "Say it out loud: dollars *per* hour. What goes on the bottom?" |
| Forgets a hidden unit | 7 dozen gloves ÷ 7 instead of ÷ 84 | "How many single pairs is 7 dozen?" |
| Stops at the unit rate | finds per-tiger cost but not the profit asked for | "Reread the question. What did they actually ask?" |
| Rounds money too early | uses $16.3 mid-problem | "Keep the cents until the last step." |
| Answers "which" but not "how much" | "Car" with no difference | "They asked two things. Did you answer both?" |

## Variant recipes
- **A:** `$P for N items → per item` with N in 3..16 and P chosen so P/N ends in .00–.99 exactly. Easy: N = 2, 4, 5. Test-level: N = 7, 12, 15.
- **B:** two offers `P1 for N1` vs `P2 for N2`, unit rates differing by 2–50¢. Test-level: three offers, or one in dozens or months.
- **C:** `R units in D days; enough for T days with S on hand?` with two items to total, as in the two-dog problem.
- **D:** `a : b = ? : c` where c = b × k, k in 2..10.
- **E:** a recipe with 2 ingredients for 2 dishes; Part A "is X enough?", Part B "how much of Y?".
- **F:** "Someone got $X per item. Find the right price and the error." Build X from dividing by the dozen count instead of the item count.

## Worked example (original)
*A 6-pack of pens costs $4.50. A 10-pack costs $7.20. Which is the better buy, and by how much per pen?*
1. Get to 1: $4.50 ÷ 6 = $0.75 per pen. $7.20 ÷ 10 = $0.72 per pen.
2. Compare: lower price is better, so the 10-pack wins.
3. How much: $0.75 − $0.72 = $0.03, or 3¢ less per pen.
